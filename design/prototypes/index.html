Throwaway, clickable prototypes, one design round at a time. The newest round comes first.

Round 2: polarity and typed ports

Three ways to show which way each port points (gives or takes) and what it carries (its type name, like "number" or "list"). Brief: design/brief-types.md. All three start from the real app's look and share everything else: the drag rules (ports that fit turn blue, ports that don't turn grey, letting go on one refuses and says why), the hover panel's plain sentences ("Takes a number. Wire it to a port that gives one."), and a light rule editor that shows each interface port's type and refuses a mistake (open the rule + with S, then "Try joining x to y"). The plain combinators look exactly like today in all three. Every net is a real engine layout, except "Length of a list", which is laid out by hand to show a second type name. Stepping replays recorded steps; drawn wires are simple curves.

A. Arrows on the wires

Every wire gets one small arrowhead in the middle, pointing from the port that gives to the port that takes, so a typed net reads like a flow chart: numbers flow from 0 through the S chain into + and out. Empty ports carry a tiny arrow too, so you can read a port before you wire it. When a system has more than one type name, the wires are labelled with it ("number", "list"); with one name there are no labels at all. The type settings sit at the top of the palette: a Types: Required chip for strictness and a chip for each name. In the type editor you click a port in the picture, then pick Gives or Takes and a name. If that change would break wires already on the canvas, they're marked in red, the canvas slides over so you can see them, and the button becomes "Save and cut 2 wires". Switching to Required walks you through each port that's missing a direction; switching to Untyped happens straight away, with Undo. The rule grid stays the familiar triangle: pairs that can never meet are hatched, with a ✓ when every pair that can meet has a rule.

Gains: direction is visible all the time and needs no explaining; it's the most familiar picture, and the canvas barely changes. Costs: adds a mark to every wire, so dense nets get busier, and name labels are left out where they'd collide. The arrows say nothing about empty ports beyond a tiny mark, and the grid still lists pairs that could never come up.

A2. Arrows, revised

A, reworked from your feedback. Direction and type are now separate: a port gives, takes or goes either way, and separately carries a type (number, list) or any type, so a port can deal only in numbers in either direction. A wire is refused only when both ends are directed the same way, or both have a type and they differ. Each system has two settings, Directions and Types, each Off / Optional / Required: the plain combinators have both off (exactly today's app), the directed combinators have directions only, arithmetic requires both, and "Length of a list" has both optional. Wires show their type as a small label whenever the system has types. "Types" now means only number, list and so on; agent types are called agents ("New agent", the agent editor). The agent editor lists every port in a row with everything visible at once: an editable label ("What this port is for"), Gives / Takes / Either, and the type. B's system-wide sheet comes across in A's style: open it from the summary line at the top of the palette ("Directions required · types optional") or from the agent editor. It holds both settings (pointing at an option says what it would do), the types with rename, delete and a count of ports, and every agent's ports in the same rows as the editor. Pointing at a type, in the palette or the sheet, lights up its ports and wires on the canvas. Hover panels are now one compact row: the port's role, a small arrow for its direction and a chip for its type, plus at most one short line.

Changed from A: the two settings replace the single strictness level, "Untyped" became "Either" with its type still settable, port labels are editable, the sheet is new, hover panels are much shorter, and the tab and slot shapes from B are gone.

B. Tabs and slots

The type is drawn into the agent itself, like jigsaw pieces: a port that gives has a little tab sticking out, a port that takes has a matching slot cut in, and a wire always runs from a tab into a slot. The shape of the joint is the type name: round for "number", square for "list", pointed when there's no name (the directed combinators). Untyped ports stay plain, so an untyped net looks exactly like today. Everything about types lives in one Types sheet (button at the top of the palette): strictness as three cards, the names with how many ports use each, and a table of every port of every agent type for quick filling in, including "Guess from the canvas". In the editor and the table, a choice that would break a wire on the canvas is greyed out with the reason, and an "Unplug it" button, the same way ports grey out while you drag. The rule grid becomes a rectangle: rows give, columns take. Pairs that both give or both take aren't in it at all, so the directed combinators' nine rules fill a 3 × 3 grid exactly. A file that doesn't type-check isn't opened until you choose: cut the bad wire, open it untyped, or don't open it.

Gains: a port explains itself before it's wired, and "these two fit" is something you can see. The rectangle grid makes the guarantee obvious. One place to see and fix every type at once. Costs: changes Lafont's outlines, and the joints are small at normal zoom. Only a few names can have their own shape. The sheet is one more screen, and its table gets wide for agents with many ports.

C. Signs on demand

At rest a typed net looks exactly like Lafont's figures. A port's type appears as Lafont's own sign, + for gives and − for takes, coloured by its type name (teal for "number", plum for "list", black for no name), only where you need it: on empty ports and loose ends (you still have to wire them), on whatever you point at or select, on every port while you drag a wire, and on every port of a name when you point at that name in the palette (click it to keep them shown). The signs are also the controls: click one, on the canvas or in the editor, and pick a plain sentence like "Takes a number". Pointing at a choice that would break wires shows them in red first. Strictness sits with the rules, as Untyped / Optional / Required. Pointing at a level previews what it would do (Untyped strips the signs and hatching; Required shows a ? on every port that still needs a direction) before you click. The rule grid groups agent types by what their principal port does, so pairs that can never meet form two labelled hatched blocks, "both give" and "both take". A file that doesn't type-check opens frozen: the bad wire is red and nothing runs until you choose.

Gains: keeps the drawing clean and faithful; dense nets stay readable; colour appears only when you ask for it. Previews before every change. Costs: you can't see the flow of a whole net at a glance, only the part you point at, so it teaches less by default. Colour for names is a new meaning to learn. Signs are small click targets.

Try in each (round 2)

  • Load "3 + 2". Drag 0, S and + in from the palette, then drag from the new 0: ports that take a number fit, the ones that give turn grey, and hovering one says why.
  • Click + in the palette and change aux port 2 to "gives". See what it does to the wires already on the canvas.
  • Load "Length of a list" (two names, δ and ε untyped). Switch it to Required and fill in the missing ports; try guessing them from the wiring (B and C).
  • Switch "3 + 2" to Untyped: it looks like today's app again. Undo brings the types back.
  • Load "Directed commutation" and the plain "Commutation" side by side to compare a polarised system with an untyped one. In an untyped system, find where types can be turned on (A: the type editor; B: the Net menu; C: the expanded rule grid).
  • Hover the rule grid's hatched cells, and click one. Open "Net → Open file…" to load a file that doesn't type-check.
  • Switch to dark mode (top right).

Round 1: three directions

Throwaway, clickable prototypes. Every net is a real layout from the engine (design/assets/nets.json). Editing and stepping are mocked: stepping replays each example's recorded steps, and wires you draw are simple curves where the real tool would route them. All three share one drawing and gesture kit (shared/netkit.js), so they differ only in layout and interaction model.

A. Canvas-first

The net fills the whole screen. Everything else floats in small islands: examples and file actions top left, the Net / Combinator translation switch top centre, undo and theme top right, running in the middle at the bottom, layout and view bottom right. Agents, components and rules sit in a floating panel on the right. Selecting something brings up a small toolbar above it. Pointing at an active pair shows "Click to fire" and, for rules that only reconnect wires, a light preview drawn right on the net.

Second round (the chosen direction): no pointer tooltips; an inspector at the bottom left shows whatever you point at, with its actions, and stays put while you move into it. Holding Alt visibly switches active pairs from "fire" to "select". Selecting several things offers batch actions, with greyed-out ones saying why. Rules are a half grid of type × type; with more than 8 types it opens as a larger popover (try "2 × 2 with 10 agent types", or "3 + 2 with typed ports" for pairs that can never meet).

Gains: the most room for the net, and the fewest steps between doing something and seeing it. Costs: stats are reduced to a chip and the step preview is light, so it teaches less. The floating panel can cover a wide net, and editors cover part of the canvas.

B. Workbench

A docked desktop-tool layout. A menu bar at the top, a canvas toolbar grouped by kind (layout, view, selection, clear), and a right sidebar with agent types (list rows you drag from), components, rules and run stats. At the bottom are transport controls with speed presets, a "Show next step" switch that draws the next state faintly over the net (onion skin), and a bar chart of interactions per step that you can click to go back. A status bar says what each mouse button does on whatever you're pointing at. Rules open in document tabs next to the net.

Gains: everything is visible and labelled; best for the second user, who designs systems and wants rules and stats on hand. Costs: the densest of the three, and the net gets the least space. The status bar is easy to overlook, and Shift means both "add to selection" and "select the wire instead of firing".

C. Step debugger

Stepping is treated like a debugger. A transport in the top bar (start, back, step, step all, run or pause, speed with slow motion), a "What happens next" inspector on the right, and a history panel at the bottom. The inspector always answers "what will this do": idle, it lists the pairs ready to fire with what each rule does. Pointing at a pair shows Now → After and draws the preview on the canvas: dashed joins, × on agents that go away, dashed outlines for agents that get created. Pointing at a port or wire end says what a drag will do. In the history you can drag the playhead through a step frame by frame, drag it onto the dashed next dot to fire the step by hand, or click an earlier step to go back. The palette and rules move to the left so that building is on one side and watching is on the other.

Gains: teaches the most, and puts time in your hands most directly (scrubbing, going back). Costs: three panels at once, so the canvas is the narrowest. It breaks the "sidebar on the right" default. The previews of rules that create agents are drawn at the agents' future positions, which can overlap the current net.

Try in each (round 1)

  • Drag an agent from the palette onto the canvas. Click a type to open its editor and switch it to a circle; agents on the canvas change live.
  • Drag from an empty port to another one. Drag over a port that already has a wire to see it refused. Let go on empty canvas to leave a loose end, which auto layout moves to the edge.
  • Drag from a wired port to unplug that end and carry the wire.
  • Load "3 + 2 with typed ports" and drag from the loose S on the right: ports that don't fit turn grey and say why.
  • Load "A pair with no rule" to see a stuck pair. Click it to open the rule.
  • Left-drag on empty canvas to select. Right- or middle-drag to pan, scroll to zoom. Alt-click (Shift-click in B) selects an active wire instead of firing it.
  • Set the speed to 0.1× and step to watch the rewiring in slow motion.
